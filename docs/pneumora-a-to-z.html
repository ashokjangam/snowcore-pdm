PNEUMORA: the complete A to Z guide

Pneumora is an air-leak copilot for three Metro do Porto train compressors, built on the public MetroPT datasets. It watches how long the compressor keeps pumping without its usual rest, and warns when that run has gone on too long. It does not catch every failure. The count is 6 of 7 air leaks, which is 6 of 9 failures once the two oil leaks are included.

In one sentence. On compressors it has not used to choose its settings, Pneumora warns in time on 6 of 7 air leaks, about 5.8 hours before the train has to come out of service, with about one false alert every 18 healthy days. It misses the 30-minute air leak E2 and both oil leaks.

The model in the app, in plain English

Nothing was changed in order to predict every failure. The app does not predict every failure. E2, E3 and X2 have no Pneumora warning because the scored result has no warning there. That is the model, not a missing line on the chart.

What "6 of 7" means

There are 7 air leaks: F01, F02, F03, F04, E1, E2 and X1. Pneumora warned in time on all of them except E2. There are also 2 oil leaks, E3 and X2. Those are not in the 7. Counted together, that is 6 of 9 failures.

  • E2 is an air leak that lasted 30 minutes (23 Mar 2022, 14:54 to 15:24). A warning only counts if it arrives at least 2 hours before the train has to come off. That deadline was 13:24, before the leak was even logged, so a timely warning was impossible once the leak had started. Pneumora also did not warn before it.
  • E3 and X2 are oil leaks lasting about three days each. The detector never looks at oil. An empty Pneumora line on those two graphs is the correct result.

What actually changed from the previous model

The earlier leave-one-compressor-out study (section 8) failed one pass mark: too many false alerts. Its own comparison showed that adding oil temperature caused those extra alerts. Protocol v2, which is the model in the app, made two changes and then froze the pass marks before scoring:

  • It dropped oil temperature and every generic anomaly score. It keeps air-leak physics only.
  • It can retune its threshold every week from the compressor's own recent healthy running, still without using failure labels. Compressor C uses that weekly retune. A and B use one threshold, set once.

All five pass marks then passed, so the status is promoted. This is still cross-validation. All nine failures had already been seen while the rules were written. There is no untouched MetroPT data left.

How it is trained

It is not a neural network, and it is not Snowflake's anomaly detector. Snowflake native ML is a separate experiment (section below). The detector in the app is a threshold on a physical measurement, chosen without failure labels, then the choice of which measurement to trust is made on the other compressors.

1 · How the co-pilot is doing

This section is the earlier, unpromoted study. The model in the app is protocol v2, described above. It passed the false-alert budget. The numbers below did not, and they are kept so the path is visible.

What is working

  • On the four MetroPT-3 leaks it alerted on all four in time. The existing alarm caught 1. On F04 it alerted 80 minutes before the reported start, because the compressor's runs had already stretched to 3–5 minutes (normal is about 2).
  • Held out across three compressors it caught 6 of 7 air leaks; the alarm caught 3.
  • It is quiet: 57 false alerts in the held-out data against 168 for the alarm.
  • Better than chance: random-alerter test p ≈ 2×10⁻⁵ (cross-validation) and p = 0.003 (untouched 2022 data).

What is not

  • False alerts are 0.167 per healthy day against a budget of 0.143. That one gate keeps it unpromoted.
  • On the untouched 2022 data it tied the alarm on air leaks (2 of 3 each) and fired 15–20 minutes later.
  • It catches no oil leaks (0 of 2). The alarm caught both, but only 57–66 hours after they began.
  • It is early detection (about an hour after onset), not a forecast hours ahead. Section 9 shows why the data does not allow that.
  • Nine failures in total is a small sample. Treat every rate here as coarse.
Honest verdict. As a leak co-pilot it adds real value: earlier and quieter than the alarm on air leaks. As a promoted predictive-maintenance model it is not there, and more tuning on the same nine failures would be fitting to the test. The next real step is more labelled failures, not more modelling.

2 · The problem

The APU feeds compressed air to a metro train's brakes, suspension and doors. When a pipe, coupling or dryer valve leaks, the compressor works harder to hold pressure until it cannot, and the train has to be withdrawn. The operator's need, published with the dataset, is to know at least two hours before the train becomes non-operational so it can be removed in a controlled way.

The train already has a low-pressure switch (LPS). It fires when air is already low, which is often too late and very noisy: it fired 114 times in the MetroPT-3 test period, and 111 of those matched no reported failure.

3 · What we tried and what happened

First campaign: forecast before onset DROPPED
Seven approaches (LPS, duty-cycle rule, cycle/pressure rule, Isolation Forest, autoencoder, synthetic-trained booster, wrong-physics control) warned 0 of 4 leaks inside a two-hour forecast window. PCA overlapped 2 of 4 with 230 false alerts. NO_PROMOTION.
Second study: the dataset owner's protocol CHOICE
Switched to Veloso et al. (Scientific Data, 2022): caught in time means an alert between two hours before the reported start and two hours before the reported end. A physical detector, the compressor staying loaded for about an hour, caught 4 of 4 in development with 12 false alerts in 174 healthy days.
Frozen, then tested once on untouched 2022 data
MetroPT 2022 and MetroPT-2: air leaks 2 of 3 (tie with the alarm, 15–20 min later), 1 false alert in 167 healthy days. NO_PROMOTION, because it did not beat the alarm.
Third study: leave one compressor out
Pooled 9 failures across 3 compressors: 6 of 9 caught vs 5 of 9; false alerts 0.167/day vs budget 0.143. NO_PROMOTION.
Fourth study: synthetic-trained model SYNTHETIC
Trained only on healthy data plus 1,200 physics-based synthetic leaks. Caught 5 of 9 with 190 false alerts; a control trained on deliberately wrong physics did better (6 of 9, 23 false alerts). NO_PROMOTION. Synthetic data was never used to validate or score anything.
Shipped: the co-pilot
The frozen detector runs beside the alarm, labelled CROSS_VALIDATED_NOT_PROMOTED, and drafts work orders for a person to review.

5 · The signal: how long the compressor works without resting

A healthy compressor loads, fills the reservoir and rests; healthy loaded runs have a median of about 1.8 minutes. During an air leak it cannot catch up, so it works non-stop, sometimes for hours, while pressure barely rises. That one physical fact is the whole detector. Generic anomaly scores (PCA, autoencoder, Isolation Forest) reacted to changes in how the train was operated and gave 1.5–2 false alerts a day, so they were dropped.

F04: the clearest case

■ Non-stop run length (minutes, square-root scale)— Reservoir pressure (bar)■ Reported failure period

From noon the runs stretch to 3–5 minutes, above the 2.15-minute threshold, and stay there for an hour, so the co-pilot alerts at 13:10, 80 minutes before the reported 14:30 start. The alarm never fires in time.

6 · The co-pilot detector, exactly

Frozen configuration: loaded_run|static|s1|p12

Alert when the compressor stays loaded (COMP == 0) continuously and the 5-minute maximum loaded-run length stays above the unit-calibrated threshold for 12 consecutive 5-minute bins (about one hour).

StepRule
resample5 minutes
fit days21
calibration days14
threshold rulelowest threshold whose merged alerts in the calibration window are <= 1 per 7 days
merge rulean alert episode ends once flags stop for more than 30 minutes
labels used to fit or calibrateFalse
No failure labels used to fit or calibrate. Each compressor learns its own normal from its first 21 days, then sets the threshold on the next 14 days so it raises at most one alert per week there. On MetroPT-3 that gives 2.15 minutes.

7 · How it is judged

Target: merged alert in [reported start - 120 min, reported end - 120 min] (Veloso et al. 2022 protocol plus the operator's two-hour removal need).

Gates declared before scoring (untouched test):

GateRequirement
air leaks caught in time>= 2 of the external air leaks scored after calibration
pooled false alerts per healthy day<= 1/7
beats existing lps alarm on air leaksmore air leaks caught in time, or equal with >= 30 min more median margin before end
random alerter null all eventsp < 0.05
  • No recipe, threshold rule, gate or window may change after external data is loaded.
  • Events that fall before a unit's calibration end are excluded and listed.
  • If any gate fails, the status is NO_PROMOTION and is reported as such.
Bug found and disclosed. The first campaign measured the alert-merge gap from the episode start, so one long condition counted as a new alert every 30 minutes and inflated false-alert counts. Fixed in the second study; the episode now ends only once flags stop for more than 30 minutes.

9 · Why there is no hours-ahead forecast

Each failure was ranked against the same compressor's healthy periods from six hours before onset to two hours after.

  • Six to two hours before onset: ordinary in eight of nine events. Only F04 shows anything (95th–100th percentile).
  • Last two hours before onset: clear only in F04, X1 and X2 (oil temperature).
  • First hour after onset: 96th–100th percentile for six of seven air leaks.
What this means. These leaks are abrupt: a burst pipe or a stuck drain valve. Nothing logged foreshadows them hours ahead, so every attempt to alert before onset scored 0 of 4. On this data, predictive maintenance means detecting a developing failure early enough to remove the train before it becomes non-operational, which is how the operator's protocol defines it.

11 · The product

Local app (FastAPI + SQLite maintenance system)

  • What needs attention: status headline, a "Why?" panel, next action, time-to-low-air projection, last four hours of pressure.
  • Co-pilot track record: the timeline above, a per-failure zoom (F01–F04) and every prediction with its outcome. Each alert has a Replay button.
  • Maintenance: a working board (start, parts, done), parts stock and crew. Co-pilot alerts open PN-CO- draft orders.
  • Performance, Training studio, Engineering evidence.
  • Replay: a calendar with red dots for failure days and amber dots for co-pilot alert days, a time slider with failure and alert bands drawn on its track, and "‹ Alert / Alert ›" buttons that jump to the previous or next event.

Snowflake app (Streamlit in Snowflake, read-only)

  • Same five views, reading the PNEUMORA tables through the Snowpark session.
  • Same replay: event jump list, previous/next alert, day picker with a dotted month calendar, time slider and a day strip.
  • Status rules live in sis/status_rules.py. A test checks they match the local server at 40 random moments and at every co-pilot alert.
  • Work orders are a read-only copy; editing happens in the local maintenance system.
Dragging the slider. Every replay moment is re-evaluated from the readings up to that time, so dragging anywhere shows what the dashboard would have said then. To land on an alert without hunting, use the jump buttons or the marked days.

15 · What PNEUMORA does NOT do

  • It does not warn on every failure. E2, E3 and X2 have no warning. E2 was too short to score. E3 and X2 are oil leaks.
  • It does not forecast leaks hours ahead, except F04. Most warnings come after the failure is already logged.
  • It does not detect oil leaks.
  • It does not diagnose which component failed.
  • Time-to-low-air is a live pressure projection, not a validated remaining-useful-life model.
  • Work orders, costs and factory OEE are demonstration records, not this train's real data.
  • Nine failures cannot give a precise estimate of future precision or transfer to other compressors.
