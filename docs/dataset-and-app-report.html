SnowCore PdM — Dataset and App Report

Snowflake CoCo CLI Hackathon 2026 · Problem Statement 3 · Predictive Maintenance & OEE Command Center. All numbers below were measured in Snowflake on 26 September 2026.

1Problem statement and objectives

Factories lose money when machines stop unexpectedly. The machine data (OT) and the business records (IT: work orders, parts, costs) live in separate systems, so nobody can see the whole story of a stop.

What we set out to do

1. Join OT and IT
Put machine events and business records in one place, linked to each other.
2. Measure OEE honestly
Availability × Performance × Quality, calculated from real counts.
3. Warn before a stop
Rank which line is most likely to stop in the next hour.
4. Turn warnings into work
Create a work order when risk is high.
5. Explain in plain English
Answer questions about causes without inventing facts.
6. One command screen
Rank lines, show the loss, name who should act.

Section 13 scores each of these honestly against what was built.

Why this one

ReasonWhy it matters
It is realStops, alarms and package counts happened in a real factory. Nothing to argue about.
It records why a line stoppedEach interval says running, slow, waiting, broken down or planned stop, with an alarm code for faults.
It counts packages in and outOEE can be calculated from real numbers instead of made-up percentages.
Enough historyTwo years is enough to test warnings on months the model never saw.
Open licenceAllowed for a public hackathon repository.

What it does not have

No vibration, temperature or RPM sensors. No work orders, technicians, parts or costs. Alarm codes are anonymised (A_065, not "bearing failure"). No product, customer or price.

We also explored a second real dataset (CoMoPI). It describes a different factory with no shared key, so joining the two would invent a factory that never existed. We dropped it from the product.

Normal · RunningMaking packages at a good speed.
Normal · Planned stopScheduled pause (cleaning, changeover). Not counted against the line.
Mild · Running slowStill making packages, but below its best speed. Lowers Performance.
Abnormal · Broken downThe machine raised a fault alarm. This is a maintenance problem. 92,084 events, typically under a minute; 90% end within 4 minutes.
Abnormal · WaitingMachine is fine but not producing, with no fault alarm. This is a planning or supply problem. 50,149 events; 10% last longer than 17 minutes.
The biggest finding in the data: the lines lose more than three times as much time waiting (10,266 h) as broken down (3,147 h). Every breakdown in the source carries an alarm; no waiting period does. Sending the maintenance team after "downtime" as one number would point them at a problem that is mostly not theirs.

What counts as an "abnormal hour" for the warning

The warning asks one question: will more than 10% of the next hour be lost to breakdowns? About 4 in 10 hours meet that bar, so it is common, not rare.

8What we added, and how (A to Z)

Five rules the generator follows

1. Never invent an event.
Every business record sits on a real stop or a real production day. No extra failures, no extra packages.
2. Same input, same output.
No random numbers. Variation comes from a fixed hash of each record's ID, so reruns give identical rows.
3. Every guess is written down.
Each constant is stored in an assumptions table and marked "measured" or "chosen by us".
4. Every row is labelled.
Observed, derived, synthetic IT or synthetic ERP.
5. Checked after every build.
Counts, orphans, cost totals and determinism are re-checked; all must be zero-difference.

12Closing the gaps: what we built, and how well it works

Three promises were only partly met or not met. We built all three and tested each one on December 2021, a month the models never saw during training.

A · A breakdown warning for the next 4 hours

Question: at the end of each hour, will this line have a breakdown of 10+ minutes (the same rule that creates a work order) in the next 4 hours? We used the same 299 inputs and model settings as the next-hour warning and changed nothing after looking at December.

The fair test: a warning only has value if it beats simple rules a planner could apply without any model. We compared it against three:

  • Downtime continues: the line that is down now stays down.
  • Recent breakdowns: lines that broke down in the last 24 hours break again.
  • Worst line first: always watch the lines that broke down most in the past.

Of the 10% of hours each method flags, how many really had a breakdown?

Honest verdict. Across the whole site the warning is 4.5 points better than "worst line first", which is within the noise for 134 flagged hours. Inside any single line it does not beat the best simple rule: it ties on s_1 and loses on s_2, s_3, s_4 and s_5. What it has learned is mostly which line tends to break and whether it broke recently. With these data, a 4-hour warning is not better than a well-chosen rule of thumb.

B · A root-cause card for every line

For every long breakdown we checked four things, using only the real machine log: were there short stops with the same alarm in the hour before? Did the same alarm come back within 24 hours? What time of day did it start? Did the line go back to running afterwards, or sit waiting?

s_2A_00153%71% start between 06:00 and 14:00; after the repair the line waits 70% of the time instead of restarting.
s_3A_00688%63% start between 14:00 and 22:00; the line waits after 67% of repairs.
s_4A_00650%51% start between 14:00 and 22:00; median breakdown 31 minutes, about twice as long as the main alarm on any other line.
s_5A_10190%71% start after short stops with the same alarm, so there is an early sign to act on.
Why it matters: on s_2 and s_3 the lost time does not end with the repair: the line sits waiting afterwards, which is a planning or staffing question, not a maintenance one. On s_1 and s_5, short stops come before the long breakdown, which gives a window to intervene.
Limits: alarm codes are anonymised, so we cannot name the part. The time bands are fixed 8-hour windows in dataset time, not the plant's real shifts, and a high share in one band may simply reflect when the line runs most. The Cortex "Explain" button only rewrites these numbers into sentences.

C · Work orders raised automatically from the warning

When an hour is in the top 10% of that line's warning scores, the system writes a planned inspection work order. A long high-risk stretch produces one order, not dozens (8-hour cooldown). Each order carries its evidence: the score, recent downtime, the alarm with most downtime in the last 4 hours, and whether that alarm's service kit is in stock. Then we checked what really happened.

14Limits, stated plainly

  • Not connected to a real ERP or maintenance system.
  • Work orders, technicians, parts, stock and euros are scenarios built on real events.
  • Warnings cover the next hour and the next 4 hours. They do not estimate remaining machine life.
  • The data ends on 1 January 2022; the app is not a live feed.
  • We cannot prove that acting on a warning would have prevented a stop.

One-line description: real factory machine data drives a fully traceable, clearly labelled maintenance and ERP workflow inside Snowflake.
